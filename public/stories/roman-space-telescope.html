NASA’s Roman Space Telescope: What Will It Discover?

GpSpace • Updated September 18, 2026

NASA’s Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is a next-generation observatory built to survey large areas of the sky while retaining detailed infrared vision. NASA says Roman launched on August 30, 2026, aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy from Kennedy Space Center and is traveling toward an orbit around the Sun–Earth L2 point, about 1 million miles from Earth.

Why is Roman different?

A major strength of Roman is its combination of a wide field of view and infrared observations. Instead of concentrating on a tiny patch of sky, Roman is designed to make large, deep surveys. NASA describes its field of view as at least 100 times wider than Hubble’s in the relevant comparison, allowing the mission to survey huge areas efficiently.

What will Roman study?

Roman was designed to investigate some of the biggest open questions in modern astronomy. Its surveys will help scientists study dark energy and dark matter, measure large populations of galaxies, and investigate exoplanets and other astrophysical objects. Because it repeatedly surveys large regions, Roman can also find objects that change brightness over time.

Why does dark energy matter?

The expansion of the universe is accelerating, and dark energy is the name scientists use for whatever is driving that large-scale effect in the current cosmological model. Roman will make large statistical measurements that can help constrain competing explanations. A telescope does not “photograph dark energy”; instead, scientists infer its properties from how cosmic structures and distances are distributed and evolve.

What about dark matter?

Dark matter does not emit or reflect light in a way that ordinary telescopes can directly observe. Astronomers infer its presence from gravitational effects on visible matter and light. Roman’s wide surveys can help map the distribution of matter through techniques such as weak gravitational lensing and galaxy clustering, providing another way to test models of cosmic structure.

Roman and exoplanets

Roman will also help build a statistical picture of planetary systems beyond our Solar System. Its observations can reveal exoplanets through gravitational microlensing, and its Coronagraph Instrument will demonstrate technology for blocking starlight so that faint objects near stars can be studied. These observations complement other methods used to detect and characterise exoplanets.

Where is Roman going?

After launch, Roman began a journey to the Sun–Earth L2 region. From that location, a spacecraft can maintain a useful geometry with the Sun and Earth while observing deep space with stable thermal and viewing conditions. NASA describes Roman’s journey as roughly three months and about one million miles to its final orbit.

What happens next?

The launch is only the beginning of the mission. Controllers must check the spacecraft, deploy and verify its systems, and prepare the observatory for science operations. The first useful scientific results will depend on commissioning and calibration, so the most important Roman discoveries will arrive after the observatory is ready to conduct its surveys.

GpSpace takeaway

Roman is not simply another replacement for Hubble or Webb. Its value comes from surveying the universe on a very large scale with infrared sensitivity. That combination can create enormous datasets that reveal patterns across galaxies, stars and planetary systems. The scientific payoff will come from how researchers analyse those measurements and compare them with observations from other telescopes.
